Speak to the listener

D6 · Stakeholders & LifecycleGuide lines 6.2, 6.4

At a glance: Shape the answer to who is listening and what they must do with it: value, cost, risk and a recommendation for whoever owns the decision; the how for whoever builds and runs it; a written record when the decision will be questioned later.

An architect explains the same design to very different people. A finance chief deciding a budget needs value, cost, risk and a recommendation, in a few minutes. Engineers who will build and run the system need component detail, interfaces and failure handling. A data-protection officer needs data flows and controls. Giving the wrong one of these is a common trap: technically perfect, useless to the listener.

When the listener owns the decision, they still want the architect's view. A balanced list of options with no recommendation pushes the analysis back onto them. The right shape is the real options, their trade-offs in the listener's terms, and a clear recommendation backed by evidence such as eval results.

Some decisions outlive the people who made them. When new managers keep reopening a settled choice, the answer is an architecture decision record (ADR): a short document stating the context, the decision, the options considered and the consequences, kept where the next person will find it.

Change one fact

An architect must explain to a retailer's chief financial officer why the returns assistant should use retrieval over the policy library rather than loading every policy into each request. Both designs pass the eval; retrieval costs less per resolved return. The CFO will approve or reject the budget in a 20-minute meeting next week. What should the architect bring?

    Words in the stem that signal this trap

    • “the board decides in a short meeting”
    • “the CFO questions the cost”
    • “the team that will build and run it”
    • “the data-protection officer must sign off”
    • “the decision keeps being questioned by new managers”
    • “present the options” to someone who owns the decision
    Rule to remember: Ask who is listening and what they must do next. Decision owners get options in their terms plus your recommendation; builders get the how; reviewers get data flows and controls; a decision that keeps returning gets an ADR.

    Practice

    Practice 12.1 · Choose one.

    A bank's data-protection officer must sign off a mortgage assistant before launch. She asks the architect what happens to a customer's personal data once they type it into the chat. The sponsor has already approved the budget and the eval results. What should the architect prepare for her?

    1. The cost per completed application and the savings expected in the first year.
    2. The eval results, showing answer accuracy across the main question types.
    3. A data-flow diagram: where personal data goes, how long it stays and who sees it.
    4. The prompt templates and tool interface specifications, with their full version history.
    Show answer

    Answer: C. The deciding fact: the listener is the data-protection officer, and her question is about personal data. Data flows, retention and access controls are what her sign-off depends on.

    • A ❌ Works for a sponsor deciding a budget, which is already approved.
    • B ❌ Works to show quality, but accuracy does not answer where personal data goes.
    • C ✅ A data-flow diagram: where personal data goes, how long it stays and who sees it.
    • D ❌ Works for engineers changing the system, but it buries the data flow she asked about.
    Practice 12.2 · Choose one.

    A retailer's chief technology officer will own the on-call rota for a new ordering agent. In a design review she asks what happens when the payment tool times out halfway through an order, because her team must handle these failures at night. What should the architect give her?

    1. The business case for the agent: cost per order, the expected savings and the main risks.
    2. The failure path: retry rules, how duplicate payments are prevented, and the alert raised.
    3. A recommendation to approve the agent, with the eval results as the evidence.
    4. Two retry designs with their trade-offs, leaving her team to choose between them.
    Show answer

    Answer: B. The deciding fact: whatever her title, she asked an operational question for a team that will run the system. Here the technical answer is the right one.

    • A ❌ Works for an executive deciding a budget, but she asked how failures are handled.
    • B ✅ The failure path: retry rules, how duplicate payments are prevented, and the alert raised.
    • C ❌ Works when the listener owns an approval decision; she asked how the system behaves.
    • D ❌ Works when the design is still open, but here the team needs the agreed behaviour to run it.
    Practice 12.3 · Choose one.

    A city council's cabinet must choose between two designs for a residents' enquiry assistant. One resolves 81% of enquiries on the eval; the other costs 40% more and resolves 90%. The cabinet owns the budget decision and has ten minutes for it. What should the architect present?

    1. Both designs with cost, resolution rate and risk, and the architect's recommendation.
    2. The recommended design alone, so the cabinet is not distracted by alternatives.
    3. Both designs with cost, resolution rate and risk, leaving the choice fully open.
    4. The technical differences between the designs, including model and retrieval settings.
    Show answer

    Answer: A. The deciding fact: the cabinet owns a real trade-off between cost and resolution. It needs both options in its own terms and the architect's recommendation.

    • A ✅ Both designs with cost, resolution rate and risk, and the architect's recommendation.
    • B ❌ Works when only one option meets a hard constraint; here both are viable and the cabinet must weigh the trade-off.
    • C ❌ Works to show the trade-off, but a decision owner also expects the architect's view.
    • D ❌ Works for engineers, but model and retrieval settings are not what the budget turns on.
